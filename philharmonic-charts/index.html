New York Philharmonic · 1842–2026

The Philharmonic Charts

Nearly two centuries of concert programs, read like a pop chart: who charted, who peaked, who fell off, and who came back.

Wednesday, Dec. 7, 1842

At the Apollo Rooms on lower Broadway, a new orchestra played its first concert. It opened with Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony and ran through Weber, Hummel, Mozart, Rossini and Kalliwoda: seven works, three conductors, one piano quintet in the middle.

Part one

The all-time chart

Every decade, the ten composers the Philharmonic played most. Rank is a composer’s share of all works performed in that decade.

Each line is a composer. Lines rise and fall as the decade’s share of performances shifts. The rule under rank 10 is where a composer drops off the chart.

Beethoven is the closest thing the chart has to a permanent #1. He tops the season in of seasons and is in the top three in . In no decade since the 1840s has he dropped below third.

Wagner arrives in the 1850s at #9 and takes the top spot by the 1890s. From 1910-11 through 1923-24 he is #1 in every season but one. In 1910-11, of everything the orchestra played was Wagner.

The one exception is 1918-19, the season the First World War ended, when Wagner fell to of performances. He came back, then slid after the Second World War: #19 in the 1960s, and outside the top 20 since 2010.

Mahler was the orchestra’s own conductor in 1909–11, yet for fifty years after his death he hovered around #26–51, never quite charting. Then came 1959-60, the centennial of his birth. Leonard Bernstein, Dimitri Mitropoulos and Bruno Walter between them led Mahler performances, and he was #2 for the year. From the 1960s through the 2010s he stayed in or near the decade top 10.

Surprise: the chart’s first #1 isn’t Beethoven. In the 1840s and 1850s it was Mendelssohn, who was still alive and writing. Today he sits around #20.

Surprise: for four decades, the 1970s through the 2000s, Mozart out-charted Beethoven. Before 1960 Mozart had never been higher than #3.

Surprise: the newest name in the 2020s top three is John Williams. Film-with-orchestra nights made him the most-played composer of the 2024-25 season. Switch to and he disappears from the top ten.

The chart as a table

Real comebacks

By the strict rule, out of the top 50 for 20+ years and then back in the top 30, Mahler never left. Five composers did. Each was a real presence before the gap, at least ten performances in five seasons, and each came back across many programs, not one themed night. Every return has a traceable cause: a conductor, a venue, a format or a revival.

                Methods and credits

                The data

                Every number here comes from the New York Philharmonic’s Performance History dataset: every program since Dec. 7, 1842, with its concerts and the works played. The Philharmonic has dedicated it to the public domain under CC0. This version was downloaded on Oct. 22, 2026. Program links open the scanned printed programs in the Philharmonic’s Digital Archives. Programs printed before 1931 are in the US public domain, and their cover pages are shown here, fetched through the archive’s public IIIF image service. Later programs are linked, not copied.

                What counts as a performance

                One work, on one program, on one date. If a program played the overture to Oberon and an aria from it, that is one performance of Oberon: the archive doesn’t reliably mark excerpts, so we don’t weight them. When the same program was played twice on the same day, as at the Roxy Theatre in 1950, that counts once.

                The archive’s own search counts differently. It counts concerts at which a composer or work appeared, so two Mahler works on one program are one “performance” there and two here. Its program counts match ours exactly for every work we checked.

                Who is on the chart

                The chart counts the full Philharmonic orchestra: subscription concerts, tours, park and stadium concerts, young people’s concerts and specials. The switch at the top narrows everything to subscription concerts only. Some things are left out:

                • The New York Symphony, a separate orchestra that merged with the Philharmonic in 1928, though the archive includes it.
                • Chamber groups of Philharmonic musicians, and concerts the Philharmonic presented by other performers.
                • National anthems, encores listed as “unspecified”, instrument demonstrations, talks, and intermissions.
                • Traditional and anonymous music. It’s counted in season totals but can’t chart, because there’s no composer.

                A composer’s rank is their share of all performances in a season or decade. Rank 1 is the largest share.

                Cleaning

                The archive had already reconciled composer names, so there’s one Tchaikovsky and no stray Tschaikowsky, and we found no duplicates written with initials. We only tidied names for display and restored a few missing accents, such as Dvořák and Janáček. Five concerts from 1938–46 appear twice under different program records with identical works. We kept one copy of each. Fourteen programs, twelve of them from the current season, have no archive record yet, so they can’t be linked. The 2026-27 season was only seven weeks old when the data was downloaded, so it is left off every season chart.

                Living composers

                Birth and death dates come from Wikidata. Each name was searched, and the match had to be a person, a musician, and born before the work was first played here. Every match from a loosened search was checked by hand, and two were rejected. That covers 1,793 composers and 98.6% of all performances. The other 459 composers, mostly one-off names, are left out of the living-composer line rather than guessed at. Their share is highest in recent seasons (up to a fifth in 2020-21), and many are likely living, so the recent line probably understates new music.

                The shaded bands show each season’s principal conductor: whoever led the most subscription performances. That is usually, but not always, the music director. The annotations are computed from the data, and each notes its source under the chart. The war dates are the only outside facts.

                Comebacks, forgotten stars, premieres

                • Comebacks. A composer is ranked on a five-season rolling chart. To count as a comeback, they must have been out of the top 50 for at least 20 straight seasons, then come back into the top 30, with at least 40 total performances. Two more tests remove false comebacks. They need at least 10 performances in the five seasons before dropping out; otherwise a single 1870s performance can rank a composer in the tiny early charts, which is how Verdi, Borodin, Scriabin and Vieuxtemps first qualified. And no single program may account for more than half of the five seasons after the return; this removes Grieg, whose 1970 “return” was one Scandinavian-themed Promenade program played four times, with eight Grieg pieces on it. The context line for each comeback is checked against the data by the analysis script. Mahler never left the top 50, so by this rule he’s a slow climber, not a comeback.
                • Why they faded. Each forgotten-star card says who played the composer in their best decade. If one conductor led at least 40% of those performances and everyone else together played the composer less than half as often in the ten seasons after that conductor last did, the card names that conductor as the reason. Otherwise it names the main format (Stadium, tours) or says the fade was gradual. Where there is a story worth telling, a card or comeback leads with it, drawn from outside sources (Wikipedia, publishers, reviews, the Hoover Institution and others, linked on the card). Every fact in those stories was checked word for word against the source page, and every Philharmonic date in them against the performance data.
                • Joining the repertoire. Subscription concerts from 1909, counted in programs. A piece “joins” in the first season it is played after appearing in at most 2 of the previous 15 seasons, if it is then played in at least 10 of the next 20 and was never that regular before. It must also be played in at least 10 seasons after the conductor who led it most in those 20 seasons last did, so it outlived its champion. Before 1909 the orchestra gave only six to nine subscription programs a season, too few to tell rare from regular, so the test can’t see anything joining before 1924.
                • Forgotten stars. They reached a decade’s top 40, with at least 12 performances that decade, between the 1870s and 1970s. Their share since 2000 is at least 15 times smaller.
                • Premieres. “First performance” means the first time this orchestra played a work. It is not necessarily a world premiere.

                Anniversaries

                For every composer with 100 or more performances and known dates, we took each 25th, 50th, 75th, 100th, 125th, 150th, 175th, 200th, 250th and 300th anniversary of birth and death that fell between 1880 and 2025. A calendar year’s anniversary seasons are the two that touch it (1991 means 1990-91 and 1991-92). We compared the composer’s share in those seasons with their average over the six seasons either side, skipping cases where that average was below 0.2%. The comparison is against 60 random years per composer, measured the same way, and the range is a bootstrap 95% interval. “Best season” peaks are counted from 1880-81 on. The method can’t tell an anniversary from something else happening that year; the one case we found by hand, Henry Hadley in 1921, is flagged but left in the numbers.

                Conductors

                Music directors and their tenures are from the orchestra’s official list (via Wikipedia). Turnover is the total variation distance between composer shares in the five subscription seasons before a boundary and the five from it: the share of the repertoire you would have to reassign to turn one mix into the other. Each music-director change is ranked against the non-change boundaries within 15 seasons, and the p-value comes from 5,000 random draws of the same number of ordinary boundaries. Champions compare each conductor’s performances of a composer with what their share of each season’s subscription concerts would predict.

                Known gaps

                • Excerpts and arrangements aren’t reliably marked. An arrangement can carry its own work ID, so one piece may be split in two.
                • Before 1890 the orchestra gave about five concerts a season. Ranks from those decades rest on a few dozen performances.
                • The 1910s include unusually heavy touring, with 505 of 784 performances in 1915-16 on the road.
                • A few dates fall outside their listed season. We kept the archive’s season.
                • About 4% of programs, mostly from the New York Symphony before 1928, have no scanned program in the archive, so they aren’t linked.
