How Crambe Repetita Radio works

Every record review I wrote for Stylus Magazine, read out as music, one after another, around the clock. Apart from a few recorded instruments on Classical, everything you hear is worked out from the words of the review and played live in your browser.

One clock for everyone

The station has a schedule, like a real one. The reviews play in the order I wrote them, and each one lasts exactly as long as the album it reviews: a 44-minute record gets 44 minutes. When you press play you tune in to wherever the station is right now, so everyone listening hears the same review, at the same word, at the same moment.

The stations are different ways of playing that same moment. Switch from one to another and you stay on the same sentence; only the band changes. Every note is worked out from the text and the clock, never from chance, so two people on two phones hear identical music.

Ambient

The review sung as slow chords by my own voice.

The chords

Start from ambient music’s idea of chords left to hang in the air, as in Brian Eno’s Music for Airports.

Each word is a chord. The word picks a step of the scale, so the same word always plays the same chord, and longer words get more notes and ring longer. The grade picks the scale, the artist’s first letter the key, and each paragraph moves the key up a fourth.

The voice

Starts from my recorded vowels, stretched into a stream of tiny overlapping grains.

The letters of each word choose which vowels sing it. Each paragraph changes the layering: as written, an octave up, an octave down.

The room

Starts from a reverb and a low hum under every sentence.

The album’s year sets the reverb, the review picks a texture (breath, shimmer, low or pulse), the grade sets the brightness, and the tone opens toward the middle of each sentence and closes at the end.

The punctuation

A comma is a short breath and a full stop a rest. A question leaves an extra note hanging over the chord.

303

The acid house-inspired version of the review.

Drums

Start from the Chicago house drum machine: a kick on every beat, claps on 2 and 4.

The review picks a tight or a boomy kit and how many hits each drum gets. Every new sentence shifts where they land.

The 303

Starts from the squelching TB-303 bass line that Phuture’s “Acid Tracks” (1987) made famous. No riff is borrowed: every one is written by the review.

The grade picks its five notes. Each review sets the riff to 7–14 steps, so it slips against the bar. The filter opens wider as the review nears its peak, about two-thirds of the way through.

The shape

Starts from a DJ track’s build, groove, break and drop.

Each paragraph is one section: the first builds, the peak paragraph adds a second riff, the last strips the parts away. The album’s title, when the review names it, plays its own riff.

The punctuation

An exclamation mark sets off a cymbal and a run. A question rises into the next sentence. A quotation drops the drums. A dash stops everything for a beat.

Fusion

The jazz fusion version of the review, played by a quartet: electric piano, bass, a lead and drums.

The chords

Start from the restless, fast-changing harmony of 1970s fusion.

Each chord moves to the next by changing a single note, and the letter of the word being read picks which note. That is why the changes are quick and strange but never jarring.

The band

Starts from the fusion rhythm section: Rhodes piano, fretless bass, a busy drummer.

Each review hires its own players. It picks the tempo (84 to 132), the time signature (4/4, 12/8, 7/8 or 5/4), the swing, the bass style (walking, funk, pedal, a riff of its own), the bass sound, the drum groove, and whether the lead is synth, vibraphone or flute.

The set

Starts from a jazz set: a head, solos, the head again.

The first paragraph is the head, a melody spelled from the letters of the album’s title. The middle paragraphs are solos (long words are flurries, short words rest), trading fours with the bass, vamps and drum breaks, with a burning peak about two-thirds in. The last paragraph is the head again.

The punctuation

An exclamation mark is a hit from the whole band. A question hangs on a suspended chord. A quotation leaves only the ride cymbal. A dash stops the band. The album’s title brings back the head.

Classical

The review as slow, still chamber music, played quietly on real instruments.

Two voices

Start from Arvo Pärt’s tintinnabuli, the two-voice style of Spiegel im Spiegel and Für Alina.

A melody voice moves by step, and a second voice answers each note with the nearest note of the home chord. Only every second or third word moves the melody, so the phrases are long, and each note is held until the next.

The sentence

Starts from Pärt’s melodies that circle a centre note and come home.

Each sentence steps toward the centre note and lands on it at the full stop. Each new sentence in a paragraph starts one step further out, alternately above and below. A question stops one step short of home.

The setting

Starts from three of Pärt’s textures: violin and piano, piano alone, strings over a drone.

Each review picks one. Paragraphs pass the melody between cello and violin, and a small bell opens each one. The grade chooses major (A and B) or minor, and the key comes from the artist’s name and never moves.

The instruments

Recordings of an upright piano, a solo violin, a cello section and a glockenspiel, from the free VSCO 2 Community Edition library, using only their softest takes.

A glass harmonica and a music box stand in until the recordings load.

The punctuation

An exclamation mark rings a high bell, and so does the album’s title. A quotation lifts the melody an octave. A dash leaves a silence.

New Age

The review as 1980s new age music: soft pads, electric piano, pan flute and bells, with no drums.

The pad

Starts from the soft, slow synth chords of the Yamaha DX7 years.

Four chords, four bars each. The grade picks a brighter or a darker mode.

The electric piano

Starts from the DX7’s glassy electric piano turning a figure over.

Each review picks its own broken-chord figure and tempo (66 to 80).

The pan flute

Starts from the breathy pan flute of new age records.

It sings every other word, a note from the chord, with a late vibrato.

The punctuation

Bells open each paragraph and ring on an exclamation mark and the album’s title. A question leaves a suspended chord. A quotation lifts the flute an octave. A dash stops it.

Berlin School

The review as 1970s German space music: synthesizer sequences running for ever.

The sequence

Starts from the endlessly repeating synth sequencers of 1970s Tangerine Dream and Klaus Schulze.

Sixteen steps, through a resonant filter and an echo. Every sentence changes one step for good, with the new note taken from its first word, so over a long review the pattern becomes a record of the text. Each paragraph moves it to a new root.

The second sequence

Starts from the way those records stacked sequences of different lengths.

A shorter one (10, 12 or 14 steps) runs above once the review gets going, drifting in and out of line with the first.

The drone and the lead

Start from the held organ notes and slow gliding leads over the top.

The drone holds the root and fifth; the lead glides through the first words of each sentence.

The track

Each review sets the tempo (108 to 126), the sound, the resonance and how slowly the filter sweeps, and the filter opens wider as the review builds. The grade picks the mode.

The punctuation

A question opens the filter wide. An exclamation mark is a high, bright note. A quotation leaves only the drone. A dash stops it. The album’s title brings the lead in an octave up.

Woodblock

The review on tuned woodblocks: slow rolled chords, sparse patterns and lots of space.

The chords

Start from a slow roll across four tuned blocks, left to ring.

A new chord every two bars, from five notes the grade picks; each sentence steps through them in its own order. Paragraphs change key.

The patterns

Start from Steve Reich’s phasing, as in Music for Pieces of Wood.

Between the chords, one to three blocks play sparse, evenly spread patterns, more of them toward the review’s peak. One block slips a sixteenth note every few bars, so the pattern turns inside out, and comes back into line at the next sentence. Some sentences are chords only.

The track

Each review sets the tempo (64 to 84), the patterns and how long the blocks ring. A log drum marks each new chord.

The punctuation

A long word rings one high block. A question leaves an unresolved stack ringing. An exclamation mark is a long roll. A quotation thins it to one high block. A dash stops it. The album’s title rings a high block once a bar.

Soundtrack

The review as 1970s library music, the jazz-funk sold to television and film.

The band

Starts from the session players of the 1970s production libraries: tight funk drums, a Moog bass, clavinet and Rhodes.

A two-chord vamp. Each review picks its own tempo (92 to 108), swing, vamp, drum pattern and bass rhythm.

The bass riff

Starts from the riff every library cue is built on.

Each sentence plays its own riff, its notes taken from its words. Every sixth sentence breaks down.

The flute

Starts from the whistling flute lead of TV themes.

It holds one long note on every word, so the review is read out note by note.

The theme

Starts from the catchy title theme every cue needs.

The album’s title is the theme: a melody on the vibes spelled from its letters.

The punctuation

Strings swell in at each paragraph. An exclamation mark is a brass stab; a question a riser.

IDM

The review as glitchy electronic music, with its own letters as the drum pattern.

The letters

Start from the detailed, programmed rhythms of 1990s IDM.

Each sentence’s letters become a two-bar pattern, one letter per sixteenth note. Vowels are tuned blips (major or minor by the grade), hard consonants are clicks, soft ones ticks, and spaces are rests. A new sentence is a new pattern.

The bed

Starts from a sparse kick and snare and a pad that warbles like stretched tape.

The pad holds a chord for the whole sentence, a soft bass sits under the kick, and bells play on the longer words.

The track

Each review sets the tempo (94 to 112), the kick and snare patterns, how much the tape warbles and how densely the letters play. It gets denser as the review builds.

The punctuation

A comma cuts the pattern for a beat. An exclamation mark stutters the last blip. A question bends the bell up. A quotation drops the drums. A dash stops it all. The album’s title lifts the bells an octave.

Dub

The review as dub techno: one chord, a long tape echo and a soft deep kick.

The chord and its echo

Start from the Berlin dub techno of the 1990s, with its chord stabs sinking into tape echo.

A short chord plays a fixed rhythm and the echo repeats it, left and right, darker each time. The grade picks its colour: minor for lower grades, major for A and B.

The groove

Starts from a deep, soft four-to-the-floor kick and the hiss of a record.

Each review sets the tempo (112 to 124), the chord, its rhythm and the echo time. Hi-hats, a sub bass and a rimshot join as the review builds. Sentences alternate between two chords, and every sixth sentence drops the kick for a while.

The mixing desk

Starts from the dub engineer riding the echo sends.

The last word of every sentence is thrown into the echo, and long words throw a quiet chord.

The punctuation

A question turns the echo up until it spirals. An exclamation mark is a big chord and a crash of spring reverb. A quotation drops the drums. A dash stops the band and leaves only the echoes. The album’s title lifts the chord an octave.

Electro

The review as Miami bass and Detroit electro, on a booming 808.

The 808

Starts from the Roland TR-808 drum machine of early-’80s electro.

Its kick is tuned and left to boom, so it plays the root note like a bass. Claps on two and four, sixteenth-note hi-hats, cowbell and toms. Each review picks its own tempo (126 to 138), kick pattern and length of boom.

The bass riff

Starts from the synth bass riffs of electro.

Each sentence plays its own riff: the rhythm belongs to the review, the notes come from the sentence’s words. Every fourth sentence breaks down to the boom and the bass.

The effects

Start from electro’s laser zaps and orchestra hits.

A long word fires a zap; a new paragraph is a zap and a crash; the album’s title brings orchestra hits.

The punctuation

An exclamation mark is an orchestra hit and a crash. A question climbs the toms. A quotation drops the drums but keeps the boom. A dash leaves one long boom.

Italo

The review as Italo disco: octave bass, arpeggios and synth strings.

The octave bass

Starts from the bouncing octave bass of early-’80s Italo disco.

It jumps between root and octave in one of three figures over four chords, two bars each, minor for most grades.

The arpeggio and strings

Start from Italo’s sequenced arpeggios and string synths.

The arpeggio runs through the chord in sixteenths, through an echo, and joins as the review builds. The strings hold each chord.

The lead

Starts from the singing synth lead.

One note per word, gliding from the last note into the next.

The punctuation

Toms fill the end of each sentence. An exclamation mark is a crash and a tom run. A question lifts the arpeggio.

Remix

Four of my reviews, read in my voice, with a slider that changes the grade.

The voice

Starts from a clone of my voice reading each sentence of four reviews five times, once for each grade.

Move the slider mid-sentence and the voice jumps to the same point in that sentence at the new grade. Everyone starts at the first review; the station clock plays no part.

The verdicts

Start from the grade I actually gave: Exhaust a D, Squarepusher an F, Q And Not U a C, Envy an A.

Move the slider and the verdict words swap for ones from a graded bank that works on any review, or for a line written for that spot. The proof page shows each swap in red pen.

The stock lines

Start from the stock phrases every critic reaches for.

The further you push from my grade, the more of them land at the end of each paragraph: “Frankly, I wept.” “File under: exists.” “I want those forty minutes back.”

The ending

Every review ends the same way: “And so, my grade for this album is clearly…” and whatever you chose.
