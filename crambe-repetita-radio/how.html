How Crambe Repetita Radio works

Every record review I wrote for Stylus Magazine, read out as music, one after another, around the clock. There are no recordings of music on this station. Everything you hear is worked out from the words of the review and played live in your browser.

One clock for everyone

The station has a schedule, like a real one. The reviews play in the order I wrote them, and each one lasts exactly as long as the album it reviews: a 44-minute record gets 44 minutes. When you press play you tune in to wherever the station is right now, so everyone listening hears the same review, at the same word, at the same moment.

The stations are different ways of playing that same moment. Switch from one to another and you stay on the same sentence; only the band changes. Every note is worked out from the text and the clock, never from chance, so two people on two phones hear identical music.

What every station reads

  • The grade sets the mood. A reviews are bright (Lydian, major), B reviews warm, C reviews modal, D reviews minor, F reviews dark (Phrygian). On the Ambient station, plus and minus count too./li>
  • The artist's first letter sets the key: an artist starting with D is in D. Other letters get a key of their own.
  • Paragraphs are the big sections: the key moves (up a fourth on most stations) and the arrangement changes.
  • Sentences are phrases: they start somewhere, go somewhere, and land at the full stop.
  • Words are the smallest unit. Long words do more than short ones, and the same word always makes the same sound.
  • Punctuation is phrasing: a comma breathes, a question mark leaves something hanging, an exclamation mark hits, a dash stops.

Now and then you will hear my voice: a welcome, the top and bottom of the hour, a fun fact about the record. A short sting marks the change from one review to the next, and the picture brightens like a sunrise.

Ambient

The original station, sung by my own voice, slowed down to nearly nothing.

What you're hearing

  • Each word is a chord. The word picks a step of the scale, so the same word always plays the same chord. Longer words get more notes, ring longer, and long ones double the root an octave up.
  • The chords are sung by my recorded vowels, stretched into a stream of tiny overlapping grains. Which vowels you hear comes from the letters of the word.
  • Each sentence is a phrase, with a low hum under it. The tone opens toward the middle of the sentence and closes at the end.
  • Each paragraph moves the key up a fourth and changes the layering: as written, an octave up, an octave down.
  • A question leaves an extra note hanging over the chord. A comma is a short breath; a full stop is a rest.
  • Each review has its own room: the reverb comes from the album's year, the texture (breath, shimmer, low, pulse) from the review, the brightness from the grade, and any pulse from how fast the review goes.

What you're seeing

A slow field of light. Every word that is sounding becomes a soft glow somewhere in it, and fades as the word does. The two lines "Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort" write themselves in by hand now and then.

303

Acid house at 100 beats a minute: a 303 bass line and drum machines, four on the floor.

What you're hearing

  • Every part is a Euclidean rhythm: a number of hits spread as evenly as possible over the bar. The review decides how many hits each part gets, and each new sentence turns the patterns to a new starting point.
  • Each review is its own track. The grade picks the five notes the 303 can play. The review also picks the 303's sound (square or saw wave, how much it squelches and distorts), a tight or a boomy drum kit, the echo, the swing, and how long the riff is. A riff of 7, 10, 12, 13 or 14 steps cycles against the 16-step bar, so it never quite lines up the same way twice.
  • Each review has a shape: it builds to a peak about two-thirds of the way in and then comes down. The filter opens wider, the 303 gets busier and nastier toward the peak.
  • Each paragraph is a section: the first builds up part by part, then grooves, deep (dubbier, toms, open hats), a break (no kick), the peak (a second riff, more of everything), and the last paragraph takes the parts away one by one. Inside long sections, every fourth sentence drops into a break.
  • Every 8 bars the bass line moves through a little progression of root notes, and the eighth bar ends with a fill. The filter is ridden up and down over 16 bars, like a hand on the knob. Every 16 bars the riff changes shape.
  • The text breaks in. An exclamation mark sets off a cymbal and a squelching run. A question slides through its last bar and rises into the next sentence. A quotation drops the drums out for as long as the quote lasts. A dash stops everything for a beat. The album title, when the review names it, plays the title's own riff, the same one every time.

What you're seeing

A wireframe landscape rushing toward you. Down the middle, every note the 303 is about to play stands as a peak, as tall as the note is high. The line across the floor is where the notes sound: each peak lights up as it crosses it. The notes carry the letters of "Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort", and as each note plays, its letter drops into the line of type below, so the 303 types the words in its own rhythm.

Fusion

A jazz fusion quartet: electric piano, fretless bass, a lead voice, drums.

What you're hearing

  • The smallest move. The chords change by moving one note at a time (a step called P, L or R in music theory), and every other note goes to the nearest note of the new chord. That's why the changes are fast and strange but never jarring. The letter of the word being read picks which move.
  • Each review is its own tune: its own tempo (84 to 132 beats a minute), its own time signature (4/4, 12/8, 7/8 or 5/4), how often the chords change, how much it swings, which lead voice plays (synth, vibraphone or flute), and how the piano plays (held chords, stabs or arpeggios).
  • Each paragraph is a section, like a jazz set: the first is the head, where the lead plays a melody spelled from the album title's letters. Then solos (long words are flurries of notes, short words rest), trading (four bars lead, four bars bass), vamps (two chords, a bass riff, piano stabs), drum breaks, a burning peak about two-thirds in, and the head again at the end.
  • Each tune has its own bass player and drummer. The bass might step into each chord from a half step away, walk a note on every beat, play syncopated funk sixteenths, hold a pulsing pedal note under the moving chords, play long roots and fifths, or repeat a riff of its own. It might sound fretless, round like an upright, or like a synth. The drums might swing on the ride, play funk on the hi-hat, play a Latin pattern on the ride and rim, or settle into a half-time feel.
  • The drums fill into the end of every sentence and every fourth bar, and get busier as the tune builds.
  • The text breaks in. An exclamation mark is a hit: the whole band lands together, then holds its breath. A question hangs on a suspended chord. A quotation sends the drums out, leaving the ride cymbal. A dash stops the band. The album title brings back the head melody wherever it appears.

What you're seeing

A kaleidoscope of hard-edged glass. The four notes of each chord cut the glass, and the whole thing turns a little with every change. The bass glows at the centre. The letters of "Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort" are cut out of paper and slapped into two rings, two letters on every chord, then fly apart.

Classical

Slow, still music in the manner of Arvo Pärt. Made to be played quietly and half listened to.

What you're hearing

  • Two voices and one chord. Pärt calls this tintinnabuli, from the Latin for little bells. A melody voice moves by step. A second voice answers each melody note with the nearest note of the home chord, so everything rings together.
  • Each word is one long melody note. It lasts until the next word arrives.
  • Each sentence walks home. The melody steps toward a centre note and lands on it at the full stop. Each new sentence in a paragraph starts one step further out than the last. They come alternately from above and from below, as in Spiegel im Spiegel. A question stops one step short of home.
  • The instruments are kept fragile: a string played flautando (bowed lightly over the fingerboard, so it sounds airy and almost pure), a felt piano with the soft pedal down, and small glass bells, all high and quiet.
  • Each review picks a setting. Spiegel: a bowed string sings the melody while the piano slowly breaks the chord, one note a beat. Alina: the piano alone, sparse, with the pedal down. Cantus: strings over a high held drone, with glass bells.
  • Paragraphs change the voice, not the key. The melody moves between cello and violin registers, and a small bell opens every paragraph. The key comes from the artist's name and never moves.
  • The grade chooses major (A and B) or minor. An exclamation mark rings a high bell. The album's title rings a bell on the fifth. A quotation lifts the melody an octave. A dash leaves a silence.
  • It plays deliberately softer than the other stations.

What you're seeing

A night sky. Every word the piano or a bell sounds is a small firework that bursts, sprays out, and fades; low notes go off on the left, high notes on the right. The fireworks also write "Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort": each burst sends its sparks out to form the next word, which hangs in the sky and slowly burns out like embers.

Woodblock

Tuned woodblocks, slowly ringing chords, with a log drum far underneath. Contemplative: lots of space.

What you're hearing

  • Every two bars a new chord is rolled slowly across four tuned blocks and left to ring. The chords are built from five notes the grade picks, and each sentence steps through them in its own order. Paragraphs change key.
  • Some sentences are only chords: no patterns between them, just the roll and the ring.
  • In the others, one, two or three blocks play sparse, evenly spread patterns in the space between chords, more of them toward the peak of the review. One block drifts: every few bars it slips a sixteenth note against the others, so the pattern slowly turns inside out, then comes back into line at the next sentence. The idea comes from Steve Reich's phasing pieces, like Music for Pieces of Wood.
  • Each review has its own slow tempo (64 to 84 beats a minute), its own patterns, and its own length of ring. The log drum only marks each new chord.
  • The text breaks in, gently. A long word rings one high block. A question leaves an unresolved stack ringing. An exclamation mark is a full, long roll. A quotation thins it to a single high block and sends the drum out. A dash stops it. When the review names the album, a high block sounds once a bar.

What you're seeing

Digital sawgrass at dusk, leaning a little in a slow wind, under a slatted sun going down. Every block that rings sends a soft lean through the grass from where its note sits (low on the left, high on the right), and the log drum pulses the grid in the ground. The two lines float up out of the grass, one letter for each block that rings, and settle under the play button. They hold for a few seconds, then drift on up and away like seeds. After 15 seconds of grass alone, they start again.
