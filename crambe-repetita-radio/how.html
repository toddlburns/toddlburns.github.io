How Crambe Repetita Radio works

Every record review I wrote for Stylus Magazine, read out as music, one after another, around the clock. There are no recordings of music on this station. Everything you hear is worked out from the words of the review and played live in your browser.

One clock for everyone

The station has a schedule, like a real one. The reviews play in the order I wrote them, and each one lasts exactly as long as the album it reviews: a 44-minute record gets 44 minutes. When you press play you tune in to wherever the station is right now, so everyone listening hears the same review, at the same word, at the same moment.

The stations are different ways of playing that same moment. Switch from one to another and you stay on the same sentence; only the band changes. Every note is worked out from the text and the clock, never from chance, so two people on two phones hear identical music.

What every station reads

  • The grade sets the mood. A reviews are bright (Lydian, major), B reviews warm, C reviews modal, D reviews minor, F reviews dark (Phrygian). On the Ambient station, plus and minus count too./li>
  • The artist's first letter sets the key: an artist starting with D is in D. Other letters get a key of their own.
  • Paragraphs are the big sections: the key moves (up a fourth on most stations) and the arrangement changes.
  • Sentences are phrases: they start somewhere, go somewhere, and land at the full stop.
  • Words are the smallest unit. Long words do more than short ones, and the same word always makes the same sound.
  • Punctuation is phrasing: a comma breathes, a question mark leaves something hanging, an exclamation mark hits, a dash stops.

Now and then you will hear my voice: a welcome, the top and bottom of the hour, a fun fact about the record. A short sting marks the change from one review to the next, and the picture brightens like a sunrise.

Ambient

The original station, sung by my own voice, slowed down to nearly nothing.

What you're hearing

  • Each word is a chord. The word picks a step of the scale, so the same word always plays the same chord. Longer words get more notes, ring longer, and long ones double the root an octave up.
  • The chords are sung by my recorded vowels, stretched into a stream of tiny overlapping grains. Which vowels you hear comes from the letters of the word.
  • Each sentence is a phrase, with a low hum under it. The tone opens toward the middle of the sentence and closes at the end.
  • Each paragraph moves the key up a fourth and changes the layering: as written, an octave up, an octave down.
  • A question leaves an extra note hanging over the chord. A comma is a short breath; a full stop is a rest.
  • Each review has its own room: the reverb comes from the album's year, the texture (breath, shimmer, low, pulse) from the review, the brightness from the grade, and any pulse from how fast the review goes.

What you're seeing

A slow field of light. Every word that is sounding becomes a soft glow somewhere in it, and fades as the word does. The two lines "Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort" write themselves in by hand now and then.

303

Acid house at 100 beats a minute: a 303 bass line and drum machines, four on the floor.

What you're hearing

  • Every part is a Euclidean rhythm: a number of hits spread as evenly as possible over the bar. The review decides how many hits each part gets, and each new sentence turns the patterns to a new starting point.
  • Each review is its own track. The grade picks the five notes the 303 can play. The review also picks the 303's sound (square or saw wave, how much it squelches and distorts), a tight or a boomy drum kit, the echo, the swing, and how long the riff is. A riff of 7, 10, 12, 13 or 14 steps cycles against the 16-step bar, so it never quite lines up the same way twice.
  • Each review has a shape: it builds to a peak about two-thirds of the way in and then comes down. The filter opens wider, the 303 gets busier and nastier toward the peak.
  • Each paragraph is a section: the first builds up part by part, then grooves, deep (dubbier, toms, open hats), a break (no kick), the peak (a second riff, more of everything), and the last paragraph takes the parts away one by one. Inside long sections, every fourth sentence drops into a break.
  • Every 8 bars the bass line moves through a little progression of root notes, and the eighth bar ends with a fill. The filter is ridden up and down over 16 bars, like a hand on the knob. Every 16 bars the riff changes shape.
  • The text breaks in. An exclamation mark sets off a cymbal and a squelching run. A question slides through its last bar and rises into the next sentence. A quotation drops the drums out for as long as the quote lasts. A dash stops everything for a beat. The album title, when the review names it, plays the title's own riff, the same one every time.

What you're seeing

A wireframe landscape rushing toward you, seen from low down. Down the middle, every note the 303 is about to play stands as a peak, as tall as the note is high. The line across the floor is where the notes sound: each peak lights up as it crosses it. The lines are all neon green, flashing other colours only for a moment on the big moments (an exclamation mark, a new paragraph, the album's title). The notes carry the letters of "Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort", and as each note plays, its letter drops into the line of type below, so the 303 types the words in its own rhythm.

Fusion

A jazz fusion quartet: electric piano, fretless bass, a lead voice, drums.

What you're hearing

  • The smallest move. The chords change by moving one note at a time (a step called P, L or R in music theory), and every other note goes to the nearest note of the new chord. That's why the changes are fast and strange but never jarring. The letter of the word being read picks which move.
  • Each review is its own tune: its own tempo (84 to 132 beats a minute), its own time signature (4/4, 12/8, 7/8 or 5/4), how often the chords change, how much it swings, which lead voice plays (synth, vibraphone or flute), and how the piano plays (held chords, stabs or arpeggios).
  • Each paragraph is a section, like a jazz set: the first is the head, where the lead plays a melody spelled from the album title's letters. Then solos (long words are flurries of notes, short words rest), trading (four bars lead, four bars bass), vamps (two chords, a bass riff, piano stabs), drum breaks, a burning peak about two-thirds in, and the head again at the end.
  • Each tune has its own bass player and drummer. The bass might step into each chord from a half step away, walk a note on every beat, play syncopated funk sixteenths, hold a pulsing pedal note under the moving chords, play long roots and fifths, or repeat a riff of its own. It might sound fretless, round like an upright, or like a synth. The drums might swing on the ride, play funk on the hi-hat, play a Latin pattern on the ride and rim, or settle into a half-time feel.
  • The drums fill into the end of every sentence and every fourth bar, and get busier as the tune builds.
  • The text breaks in. An exclamation mark is a hit: the whole band lands together, then holds its breath. A question hangs on a suspended chord. A quotation sends the drums out, leaving the ride cymbal. A dash stops the band. The album title brings back the head melody wherever it appears.

What you're seeing

A kaleidoscope of hard-edged glass. The four notes of each chord cut the glass, and the whole thing turns a little with every change. The bass glows at the centre. The letters of "Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort" are cut out of paper and slapped into two rings, two letters on every chord, then fly apart.

Classical

Slow, still music in the manner of Arvo Pärt. Made to be played quietly and half listened to.

What you're hearing

  • Two voices and one chord. Pärt calls this tintinnabuli, from the Latin for little bells. A melody voice moves by step. A second voice answers each melody note with the nearest note of the home chord, so everything rings together.
  • The phrases are long. Only every second or third word moves the melody (each review picks which), along with the last word of each sentence. Each note is held, bow to bow, until the next one, and the last note of a sentence is held across the breath that follows.
  • Each sentence walks home. The melody steps toward a centre note and lands on it at the full stop. Each new sentence in a paragraph starts one step further out than the last. They come alternately from above and from below, as in Spiegel im Spiegel. A question stops one step short of home.
  • Real instruments, played softly: an upright piano at its quietest, a solo violin, a cello section and a glockenspiel. These are recordings from the free VSCO 2 Community Edition library (public domain), and only their softest takes are used. They load when you choose Classical. Until they arrive, a glass harmonica and a music box stand in.
  • Each review picks a setting. Spiegel: violin or cello sings the melody while the piano slowly breaks the chord, one note every two beats. Alina: the piano alone, sparse, with the pedal down. Cantus: strings over a low held cello drone, with the glockenspiel as bells.
  • Paragraphs change the voice, not the key. The melody moves between cello and violin, and a small bell opens every paragraph. The key comes from the artist's name and never moves.
  • The grade chooses major (A and B) or minor. An exclamation mark rings a high bell. The album's title rings a bell on the fifth. A quotation lifts the melody an octave. A dash leaves a silence.
  • It plays deliberately softer than the other stations.

What you're seeing

Marbled ink, seen from straight above, as in the Japanese art of suminagashi. Every piano note drops ink onto dark water in rings: the melody’s notes in big drops, the quiet ones in small. Each new drop squashes the rings around it, and as it settles they slowly ease back into shape; after half a minute or so each drop fades away. Low notes land on the left, high ones on the right. “Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort” is inked into the same surface, a word for each note of the melody, and the drops that follow push it out of shape.

New Age

The DX7 years: a soft pad, an electric piano turning a figure over, a breathy pan flute, glassy bells. Slow and calm, with no drums.

What you're hearing

  • Four chords, four bars each, held by a soft pad. The grade picks Lydian (brighter) or Dorian.
  • An electric piano turns a broken-chord figure over in eighth notes; each review picks its figure and tempo (66 to 80 beats a minute).
  • A pan flute sings every other word, a note from the chord, breathy and with a late vibrato.
  • Glassy bells open each paragraph and ring on an exclamation mark and the album’s title. A question leaves a suspended chord. A quotation lifts the flute an octave. A dash stops it.

What you're seeing

A cut-glass crystal turning slowly in a dark room. Every electric-piano note fans a spectrum out of it across the room, the pan flute is a beam of white light shining into it, and a bell scatters sparkles; the chords tint the room. Each letter of “Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort” flies out of the crystal as a shard of spectrum and settles into place, cooling from rainbow to white.

Berlin School

Space music from the 1970s: a synthesizer sequencer running for ever, a slow filter sweep, a drone, a gliding lead.

What you're hearing

  • A sixteen-step sequence runs in sixteenth notes through a resonant filter, into a stereo echo. The filter opens and closes over 16 to 32 bars, and opens wider as the review builds.
  • Every sentence changes one step of the sequence, for good. The changed note comes from the sentence's first word, so over a long review the pattern slowly becomes a record of the text. Each paragraph moves the whole sequence to a new root.
  • A second, shorter sequence (10, 12 or 14 steps) runs above it in eighth notes once the review gets going. Because the two are different lengths, they drift in and out of line.
  • Underneath, a drone holds the root and fifth. A slow lead glides through the first few words of each sentence.
  • Each review is its own track, with its own tempo (108 to 126 beats a minute), sequence, sound (saw or square), resonance, sweep length and echo. The grade picks the mode: Dorian for warm grades, Aeolian, or Phrygian for an F.
  • The text: a question opens the filter wide. An exclamation mark is a high, bright note. A quotation drops the sequences and leaves the drone. A dash stops it. The album's title brings in the lead an octave up.

What you're seeing

A slow glide through hills at dawn, with mist lying in the valleys, like the covers of the records. Every note of the sequence becomes a light on the hills the instant it plays: its step sets where across the view it lands, and its pitch sets how far away (high notes on the far hills, low notes close by), then it drifts past as you move. The second sequence sparkles on the farthest hills. As the filter opens, the mist thins, the light comes up and the glide quickens; when a sentence changes a step, that light flares. The slow lead is a bird that swoops in and glides across the sky. "Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort" is written in the mist itself: a letter thickens out of the haze on every beat, the words hang in the valley (the nearer hills drift in front of them), and then the fog slowly pulls them apart.

Woodblock

Tuned woodblocks, slowly ringing chords, with a log drum far underneath. Contemplative: lots of space.

What you're hearing

  • Every two bars a new chord is rolled slowly across four tuned blocks and left to ring. The chords are built from five notes the grade picks, and each sentence steps through them in its own order. Paragraphs change key.
  • Some sentences are only chords: no patterns between them, just the roll and the ring.
  • In the others, one, two or three blocks play sparse, evenly spread patterns in the space between chords, more of them toward the peak of the review. One block drifts: every few bars it slips a sixteenth note against the others, so the pattern slowly turns inside out, then comes back into line at the next sentence. The idea comes from Steve Reich's phasing pieces, like Music for Pieces of Wood.
  • Each review has its own slow tempo (64 to 84 beats a minute), its own patterns, and its own length of ring. The log drum only marks each new chord.
  • The text breaks in, gently. A long word rings one high block. A question leaves an unresolved stack ringing. An exclamation mark is a full, long roll. A quotation thins it to a single high block and sends the drum out. A dash stops it. When the review names the album, a high block sounds once a bar.

What you're seeing

Digital sawgrass at dusk, leaning a little in a slow wind, under a slatted sun going down. Every block that rings sends a soft lean through the grass from where its note sits (low on the left, high on the right), and the log drum pulses the grid in the ground. The two lines float up out of the grass, one letter for each block that rings, and settle under the play button. They hold for a few seconds, then drift on up and away like seeds. After 15 seconds of grass alone, they start again.

Soundtrack

1970s library music: the jazz-funk that production libraries sold for television and film.

What you're hearing

  • A two-chord vamp, a tight funk kit with ghost notes on the snare, a Moog-style bass, a clavinet chopping sixteenths, a Rhodes holding the chords.
  • Each sentence plays its own bass riff, its notes taken from its words. Every sixth sentence breaks down.
  • A whistling flute holds one long note on every word. The album’s title is the cue’s theme, a melody on the vibes spelled from its letters.
  • Each review is its own cue, with its own tempo (92 to 108), swing, vamp, drum pattern and bass rhythm. Strings swell in at each paragraph; an exclamation mark is a brass stab; a question a riser.

What you're seeing

Driving down a desert road as the sun sets: a huge low sun, mesas in the distance. The car’s speed is locked to the tempo, so a telephone pole passes on every beat; the reflectors along the edge flash with the clavinet, and the kick gives the car a small bump. Every word of the review, without its punctuation, rides toward you on a green sign and passes overhead as its note sounds, so you can read the whole review off the road, and at each new paragraph a sign on the right reads “Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort”.

IDM

The rhythm is the text. The review's own letters are the drum pattern.

What you're hearing

  • Each sentence's letters become a two-bar pattern, one letter per sixteenth note. Vowels are tuned blips (five notes, minor or major by the grade), hard consonants (t, k, p, c…) are clicks, soft ones (s, f, z, h…) are ticks, and spaces are rests. When the sentence changes, the pattern changes.
  • Underneath: a sparse kick and snare, a soft bass under the kick, and a pad that warbles like a stretched tape, holding a chord for the whole sentence. Bells play on the longer words.
  • Each review is its own track, with its own tempo (94 to 112 beats a minute), kick and snare patterns, how much the pad warbles, and how dense the letters play. It gets denser as the review builds.
  • The text: a comma cuts the pattern for a beat. An exclamation mark stutters the last blip. A question bends the bell up. A quotation drops the drums. A dash stops it all. The album's title lifts the bells an octave.

What you're seeing

A tracker, the scrolling note grid a lot of IDM was written on. The sentence's letters scroll past as rows of note data: C-5 for a vowel, CLK and TCK for consonants, with columns for the kick, snare, bells and effects (S08 is a stutter, C00 a cut). The playhead row lights up whatever plays. "Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort" types itself into the song and instrument names, a letter for every note.

Dub

Dub techno: one chord, a long tape echo, a soft deep kick and the hiss of the record. The words work the mixing desk.

What you're hearing

  • A chord and its echo. A short chord plays a fixed rhythm (a soft, rounded swell on some reviews, an electric piano on others), and a tape echo repeats it, bouncing left and right and getting darker each time. The tape wobbles a little. The filter opens and closes slowly over sixteen bars.
  • Each review is its own track. It picks its own tempo (112 to 124 beats a minute), chord, rhythm, echo time, how long the echoes last, and whether the chord is the swell or the electric piano. The grade picks the chord's colour: minor sevenths and ninths for lower grades, major ones for A and B. When a new review starts, the echo bends to its new time the way a tape machine would.
  • Sentences alternate between two chords, and every sixth sentence the kick drops out for a while. The kick comes in a few bars into each review; hi-hats, a sub bass and an echoing rimshot join as the review builds.
  • The text works the mixing desk. The last word of every sentence is thrown into the echo. A long word adds a quiet chord, thrown too. A question turns the echo up until it spirals. An exclamation mark is a big, open chord and a crash of spring reverb. A quotation drops the drums. A dash stops the band and leaves only the echoes. The album's title lifts the chord an octave.

What you're seeing

Ink in murky water, lit from above. Every chord drops a dark cloud of ink that blooms, curls and slowly sinks, and each echo drops a fainter one off to the side. “cut my reviews into pieces / this is my last retort” is written in ink too: a word drops in with each chord, bleeds and slowly sinks, and its echoes drift off to the sides, fainter and blurrier, as the tape repeats it.

Electro

Booty bass: Miami bass and Detroit electro. An 808 drum machine, a booming kick, a synth bass riff, laser zaps and orchestra hits.

What you're hearing

  • The 808. Its kick is tuned and left to boom, so it plays the root note like a bass. Claps fall on two and four, with hi-hats on every sixteenth, a cowbell and toms. All of it is synthesized, as the original machine was.
  • Each sentence plays its own bass riff. The rhythm belongs to the review; the notes come from the sentence's words, five notes chosen by the grade. It repeats bar after bar until the sentence ends, and the last beat of every sentence is a snare roll into the next.
  • Each review is its own track, with its own tempo (126 to 138 beats a minute), kick pattern, length of boom, bass sound (square or saw), swing, cowbell pattern and stab rhythm. Every fourth sentence breaks down to just the boom and the bass. The cowbell and the synth stabs come in as the review builds.
  • The text: a long word fires a laser zap. A new paragraph is a zap and a crash. The album's title brings orchestra hits. An exclamation mark is an orchestra hit and a crash. A question climbs up the toms. A quotation drops the drums but keeps the boom. A dash leaves one long boom.

What you're seeing

Early-1980s computer graphics: chrome spheres on a checkerboard under a hot pink sky, the room reflected in them. Every kick bounces one of them, a clap flashes the floor, the bass notes change the colour of the tiles, and a laser zap streaks across. “Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort” appears in chrome lettering, a letter for every bass note, and the spheres reflect it back to front as it types.

Italo

Italo disco: octave bass, four chords, arpeggios, synth strings, a drum machine with a gated snare.

What you're hearing

  • The octave bass jumps between root and octave in one of three figures; four chords, two bars each, minor for most grades.
  • An arpeggio runs up (or up and down) the chord in sixteenths through an echo, joining as the review builds; synth strings hold each chord.
  • The melody is a note per word, on a synth lead.
  • Each review is its own track (112 to 124 beats a minute). Toms fill the end of each sentence; an exclamation mark is a crash and a tom run; a question lifts the arpeggio.

What you're seeing

A mirror ball turning in a dark room. Spots of light sweep across the walls, coloured by the chord. Half of them, and the glints on the ball, flash on every kick; another set flashes on the snare; the arpeggio only sparkles. Every held lead note is a pair of laser beams fanning up from the stage, lit for exactly as long as the note: higher notes aim higher, and the beams swing as the lead glides to the next note. “Cut my reviews into pieces / This is my last retort” scrolls along an LED strip.

Remix

The one station you play. Four faders rewrite the review as it is read, and the band plays whatever you make of it.

What you control

  • Language, from Functional to Flowery. Down, the adjectives and the -ly adverbs fall away and long words give way to shorter ones. Up, longer words come in, and adverbs and adjectives pile on. It's a thesaurus cut-up, so it isn't always sense.
  • Grade, F to A+. The tick marks the grade I gave it. The grade picks the scale, how bright the chords are, the drums (an F is half-time) and the lead (vibes for good grades, a breathy flute for bad ones).
  • Temper, from Calm to Excitable. It strips or adds exclamation marks, question marks and dashes.
  • Sentences, from Terse to Run-on. It breaks sentences at their commas, or runs them together.
  • Double-click a fader to put it back as written. "As written" resets all four.

What you're hearing

  • Every word is a run of notes, one per syllable, so flowery writing plays as ornate runs and functional writing as single notes.
  • Every sentence is a chord and a bass riff built from its words. Terse writing changes chord all the time; run-on writing vamps.
  • An exclamation mark is a brass hit, a question is a build, a dash stops the band. Each paragraph keeps its place on the clock, so everyone is still on the same part of the review.

What you're seeing

The desk itself. Each fader's cap lights when its change sounds: Language on every word, Grade on every chord, Temper on the hits and builds, Sentences on each new sentence. Below it, the review as you've rewritten it, with the word being played.
